Impulse Buying and ADHD: Understanding Your Spending Triggers

Published: October 19, 2026 · By Monica Becerra · 8 min read

It's almost midnight. You opened your phone to answer one message, and somehow there's an order confirmation in your inbox. The thing you bought isn't really the story. What happened in the ten minutes before it usually is.

In short: ADHD makes impulse buying more likely because of differences in reward processing, a stronger pull toward immediate rewards, and emotions that arrive faster. Most impulse purchases are triggered by boredom, stress, tiredness, or novelty. Noticing the feeling before each unplanned purchase, making if-then plans, and adding a wait between the urge and the purchase all help. It isn't a willpower problem

Impulse buying gets talked about like a character test you keep failing. For a lot of ADHD brains it works more like a wiring difference. Research on the brain's reward pathways has found differences in dopamine signaling in adults with ADHD, which helps explain why something new, fast, and rewarding pulls so much harder than it seems to for other people.

Time plays a part too. A meta-analysis comparing people with and without ADHD found a consistent tendency to discount future rewards more steeply, which means money you might have next month simply feels less real than the thing you could have tomorrow. And emotions tend to arrive faster and bigger. Researchers call this emotion dysregulation, and while it isn't in the official diagnostic criteria, it shows up in a large share of adults with ADHD.

Put those three together and an impulse purchase stops looking like a lack of discipline. It looks like a brain doing exactly what it was built to do, inside an online store that was designed to take advantage of it.

The purchase is the last step, not the first

Consumer researchers have described the buying impulse as a sudden, strong urge to buy something right now, one that arrives with very little deliberation and a lot of feeling. That feeling part matters. Most impulse purchases are the end of a chain that started somewhere else: a hard conversation, a boring afternoon, a night when you were too tired to do anything but scroll.

If you only look at what you bought, you'll keep trying to fix the wrong thing. Deleting the shopping app helps for a week. Understanding why you opened it at 11:40pm helps for much longer.

The triggers that come up again and again

Boredom is a big one. When a task is dull and your brain is hunting for stimulation, browsing and buying are a quick, reliable source of something new. Hard emotions are another: stress after work, a message that stung, the low hum of anxiety you can't quite name. A purchase gives you a moment of relief and a small sense of control, which is exactly why it's so easy to reach for.

Then there's tiredness. Late at night, the part of you that would normally ask “do I need this?” is the first to clock out, and checkout pages never close. And finally there's novelty. A new interest can feel urgent in a way that's hard to explain to anyone else, and suddenly you need the gear, the books, and the starter kit before the excitement fades. Sometimes it fades three days after the box arrives.

None of these are bad on their own. Boredom, stress, tiredness, and excitement are just signals. The trouble starts when buying becomes the default way of answering them.

How to find your own triggers

You don't need a spreadsheet for this. For the next couple of weeks, every time you buy something you didn't plan to, write down three small things: what it cost, the time, and one word for how you felt right before. “Headphones, $60, 11pm, wired.” That's a complete entry.

After two weeks, read it all back in one sitting. Most people find that their impulses cluster in ways they didn't expect. Maybe it's Sunday evenings. Maybe it's the hour after a stressful call, or every time they feel left out. Once you can see the pattern, you're no longer fighting a mystery. You're responding to something specific.

Put some space between the urge and the purchase

The goal isn't to never buy anything on impulse again. It's to give the slower part of your brain a chance to catch up. One of the most reliable tools in behavior research for this is the implementation intention, a simple if-then plan you decide in advance. Across many studies, people who made specific if-then plans were noticeably more likely to follow through than people who only set the goal.

For spending, that might sound like: if I'm on a checkout page after 10pm, then I save it to a list and close the tab. If I want to buy something right after a hard conversation, then I text a friend first. The plan works because you're not making a decision in the moment. You already made it, on a calmer day.

It also helps to make the price feel real. Because future money is easy to discount, “$85” can feel like nothing. “Almost a full day of work” or “seventeen coffees” lands differently, and that small shift is often enough to turn “buy now” into “maybe later.”

When it happens anyway

Sometimes it will happen anyway. That isn't the system failing, that's being a person. The part worth protecting yourself from is the spiral afterward, where shame about one purchase turns into avoiding your banking app, which turns into not knowing where you stand, which makes the next stressful moment, and the next impulse, more likely.

Check the return window. Add it to your log with the feeling, like any other entry. Then let it go. A purchase you can learn from is useful information, not proof that you're bad with money.

Quick answers

Why do people with ADHD impulse buy more?

Research points to differences in the brain's dopamine reward pathways, steeper discounting of future rewards, and emotion dysregulation. Together they make an immediate purchase feel more rewarding and future money feel less real. It isn't a lack of willpower.

What are the most common impulse spending triggers?

Boredom and the search for stimulation, hard emotions like stress or rejection, tiredness late at night, and the excitement of a new interest. The trigger usually happens before the purchase, so noticing how you felt right before buying is the best way to find yours.

How can I stop impulse buying with ADHD?

Aim for space, not perfection. Write down the feeling behind each unplanned purchase for two weeks, decide if-then plans in advance (for example, if I'm on a checkout page after 10pm, I save it to a list and close the tab), add a waiting period before buying, and translate prices into hours of work.
