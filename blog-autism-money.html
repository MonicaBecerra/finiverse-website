Autism and Money: Routines, Sensory Overload and Literal Language

Published: October 26, 2026 · By Monica Becerra · 9 min read

The self-checkout announces an unexpected item in the bagging area. The light above you hums at a pitch nobody else seems to hear, the queue behind you is growing, and a screen asks whether you would like to round up for charity. You came in for milk. Somewhere between aisle three and this machine, your whole afternoon got spent. For many autistic people, money is not mostly a maths problem. It is a sensory, language and predictability problem that happens to involve numbers.

In short: Autistic people often meet money barriers that standard advice never mentions: loud and unpredictable shops, banking language full of figures of speech, and systems that punish a disrupted routine. A survey of autistic adults in Australia found that almost half struggled to make ends meet, compared with about one in three people in the general population. The most useful fixes are practical and specific: make shopping predictable, ask for money information in writing and in plain words, and give your special interests an honest line in the budget.

The shop is the hardest part of the budget

A supermarket is where many of us actually spend money, and it is also one of the most punishing sensory places there is. In a UK study, MacLennan and colleagues ran online focus groups with 24 autistic adults and asked where in public life the senses are hardest to manage. Supermarkets, restaurants and cafés, busy town centres, public transport and health care settings were commonly described as disabling. The authors summed up what makes a place workable as six linked ideas: the sensory environment, space, predictability, understanding, adjustments and recovery.

Read that list as a shopper rather than a researcher. Predictability means knowing the layout, the queue and the payment step before you arrive. Recovery means somewhere to come down afterwards. When both are missing, you pay in energy first and in money second: the quick wrong item grabbed to escape, the delivery fee, the trip abandoned and a takeaway ordered instead. That last chain is my reading of the findings, not something the study measured.

So treat the shop as something to design. Go at the same quiet hour each week. Write the list in the order of the shelves so you never wander. Wear headphones or sunglasses if they take the edge off. Decide which till costs you less noise, the staffed one or the machine, and decide at home how you will pay so the card reader holds no surprises. When the week is already full, order online and call it a budget decision, because it is one.

What does “pending” actually mean?

Banks speak in figures of speech. A payment is pending. A card is frozen. Funds clear, a transfer bounces, an account is overdrawn, a hold is placed. Read literally, none of it describes what is happening, and a message saying your payment may be reversed leaves you guessing about what to do next. If you take words at face value, the problem is the vocabulary, not you.

The research asks for some care here. A meta-analysis of 41 studies by Kalandadze and colleagues found that, on average, autistic participants understood figurative language less well than non-autistic participants, a moderate difference (Hedges’ g of 0.57), and metaphors were harder than irony or sarcasm. But the gap was small and not statistically significant when the groups were matched on language ability, and age did not predict it. That is a pattern across groups. It tells you nothing about whether you, personally, find a bank letter confusing.

Whatever your own profile, you are allowed to ask for plain words. Ask the bank to explain a term in one sentence. Ask for the next step as an action with a date, such as what to do and by when. Ask for it in writing so you can read it again without the pressure of a phone call. A good adviser can answer those questions, and if they cannot, that is their gap to close.

Routines hold the budget up, and prices don’t care

A routine is a money system whether or not you call it one: the same shop, the same brand, the same day for the same bill. It works until a price rises, a product disappears or a subscription renews at a new rate, and then a small change can knock over the whole week. That is not a character flaw. It is what happens when a system relies on things staying the same.

The wider picture is harder than any single routine. Cai, Hall and Pellicano surveyed 191 autistic adults aged 18 to 83 in Australia and found that almost half felt it was a struggle to make ends meet, compared with about a third of the general Australian population. What predicted financial wellbeing was much the same as for everyone else: income, and saving instead of borrowing for everyday costs. Income mattered, which means that money pressure is not simply a matter of habits.

The companion study listened to 21 autistic adults. They described financial wellbeing as having enough for the basics, a safety net for unexpected bills and freedom from money worry, and many felt it was out of reach. Unstable income was a key barrier, and several budgeted and saved where they could, partly because they could not count on family or friends. Financial insecurity also took a toll on their mental health. In practice, that makes a small buffer less a luxury than the thing that keeps a changed routine from becoming a crisis.

Your special interest is allowed a line in the budget

Money spent on a deep interest is often the best-spent money in someone’s month. It is where the joy, the expertise and sometimes the friendships live. The trouble starts when that spending has no place in the plan, so it only shows up as a shock on the statement and arrives with shame attached.

Give the interest a named amount, chosen on a calm day, and treat anything beyond it as a conversation with yourself rather than a verdict. If a bigger purchase tempts you, a wait of a few days is a choice, not a punishment, and you get to decide whether it suits you. It is also worth noticing the purchases that follow a hard sensory day. Sometimes the thing you bought was recovery, and seeing that pattern without judging it is more useful than a rule you will break by Thursday.

When the help isn’t built for you

If money does tip into debt, the support around it often assumes a brain that opens letters on arrival and phones on request. In its 2025 report, the UK charity StepChange surveyed 278 neurodivergent people in problem debt and interviewed 30 people with ADHD, autism or both. Ninety-seven percent said being neurodivergent made managing debt harder at least sometimes. About 64 percent had not contacted their creditors for help, and the barriers they named were feeling overwhelmed (73 percent), anxious (72 percent) and stigma (60 percent).

Only 32 percent of those who reached an advice service told it they were neurodivergent. If you do get in touch, saying that you are autistic is a legitimate part of the request, and so is asking for adjustments: written communication, a named contact, more time. You are not asking for a favour. You are describing how the process needs to work for you.

Quick answers

How does autism affect managing money?

It varies a lot from person to person. Common barriers include sensory overload while shopping, banking language that relies on figures of speech, routines that break when prices or services change, and systems that assume you can handle phone calls and sudden deadlines. Research with autistic adults in Australia also found that almost half struggled to make ends meet, and that income mattered as much as spending habits.

Why is shopping so overwhelming for some autistic people?

Supermarkets combine noise, bright lighting, crowds and constant small decisions, and they are hard to predict or leave. In a UK focus-group study of 24 autistic adults, supermarkets were commonly described as disabling places. Going at a quiet hour, writing a list in shelf order and deciding how you will pay beforehand can lower the load.

Why is bank language so confusing if you take words literally?

Banks use many figures of speech, such as a payment that is pending, a card that is frozen or a transfer that bounces. Research suggests autistic people on average understand figurative language less well, though the gap shrinks when groups are matched on language ability. Either way, you can ask a bank to explain a term in one sentence and to give the next step in writing, with a date.
