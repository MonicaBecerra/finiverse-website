Neurodivergence and Debt: Why It Happens and How to Get Out Without Shame

Published: October 20, 2026 · By Monica Becerra · 11 min read

The envelope has been on the kitchen counter for eleven days. You know roughly what's inside, and that's exactly why it's still closed. Every day it sits there, the number inside gets a little bigger, and so does the knot in your stomach when you walk past it. If that feels familiar, this one is for you.

In short: Debt isn't an ADHD problem or an anxiety problem. People with anxiety, depression, OCD, ADHD, bipolar disorder and other mental health conditions are all more likely to be in problem debt, and many autistic adults struggle to make ends meet. The routes differ: avoiding the mail, admin that stops when energy runs out, urges and missed dates, tight income, hidden costs in the numbers. The way out starts the same way for everyone: one look without judgment, stopping the fee leaks, and free, non-profit debt advice.

It isn't one diagnosis

When people write about money and neurodivergence, ADHD tends to take up all the space. The data tells a wider story. In England, the Money and Mental Health Policy Institute analyzed a nationally representative survey and found that almost one in five people with a mental health problem were in problem debt, compared with one in twenty people without one. Every condition they looked at had higher rates, from about two and a half times as likely for people with panic disorder to almost six times as likely for people with OCD.

How intense things get matters more than the label. Among people with the mildest symptoms of anxiety and depression, 4% were in problem debt. Among people with severe symptoms, 22% were. The report also makes a point I wish more people understood: you are most likely to fall behind exactly when you are least able to deal with it. And many of us don't fit neatly into one diagnosis anyway. Two people with the same label can struggle in completely different ways.

Anxiety, OCD and the envelope you can't open

With anxiety, the problem often isn't spending. It's looking. Behavioral economists call the urge to look away from bad financial news the ostrich effect: in one study, investors checked their accounts more often when markets were rising and less often when they were falling. If a balance already makes your chest tighten, leaving the envelope closed feels like protection.

The unfair part is that avoidance has a price. While the envelope stays closed, interest keeps running and fees keep landing. The debt grows, so the dread grows, so looking gets harder. OCD can pull in the other direction, into checking and rechecking or worrying for hours about a single payment, which is just as exhausting. Neither one means you don't care. Usually it means you care so much that it hurts.

Depression: when the admin just stops

Depression often looks like nothing from the outside. Bills go unpaid, not because of a shopping spree, but because opening the app, finding the password and making the call each feel impossible. One person quoted in the institute's report described losing the ability to organize everyday tasks, getting confused about whether they had paid something, and being unable even to pick up the phone to explain.

A review that pooled 65 studies found that people with unsecured debt, like credit cards and personal loans, had nearly three times the odds of depression. The authors said the direction is hard to pin down and most likely runs both ways: debt wears down your mood, and low mood makes debt harder to manage.

If money worries are making you feel hopeless or unsafe, please talk to someone today: a crisis line, your doctor, or emergency services. Debt can be sorted out. You matter far more than any balance.

ADHD and bipolar disorder: when urges and time work differently

With ADHD, debt tends to build slowly. A Swedish study that linked national health records with credit data found that adults with ADHD started out with normal borrowing and default rates. Then the gap widened year after year, and by around age 40 their risk of default was more than six times that of the general population, even after accounting for education, income and other conditions. Late fees from time blindness, bills forgotten the moment something else grabs your attention, and purchases that feel urgent at 11pm all add up. I wrote more about that pull in the article on impulse buying.

It's worth being honest about the research here too. A Dutch study found that adults with more ADHD symptoms reported more impulsive buying and more avoidant money decisions, but much of that link was explained by personality, low mood and life circumstances. Bipolar disorder can bring a different pattern. One person in the institute's report described buying extravagant things on credit during a hypomanic episode, and then, once depression set in, worrying obsessively about what they owed and being unable to ask for help.

Autism, dyscalculia and the costs nobody sees

For many autistic adults, the struggle starts with income rather than spending. In an Australian study of 191 autistic adults, almost half said it was a struggle to make ends meet, compared with about a third of the general population. Income and everyday money habits, like saving and not borrowing to cover daily costs, were what predicted how people felt about their finances. When income is tight, debt can arrive simply from trying to cover the basics.

Numbers are another hidden cost, and not only for people with dyscalculia. In a national survey in the United States, only about a third of people seemed to understand how interest compounds or how credit cards work, and the researchers estimated that up to a third of the fees and charges paid by the least knowledgeable came from that gap. If percentages already feel like a foreign language, the true cost of paying only the minimum, or of a “buy now, pay later” plan, stays hidden until the statement arrives.

Start by looking, not by fixing

Whatever your route into debt, the first step out is the same, and it isn't a payoff plan. It's one honest look, kept small on purpose. Pick a time, give it twenty minutes, and if you can, ask someone to sit with you. Plenty of people find hard tasks easier with a body double, someone who is simply there, not judging or fixing anything. Open the envelopes and the apps and write down four things for each debt: who it's with, how much you owe, the minimum payment, and the due date. That's all. No math, no plan, no verdict.

Then stop the leaks. If your bank allows it, set up automatic minimum payments so a forgotten date doesn't add another fee, and cancel the subscriptions you don't use. If you're already behind, contact the lender and ask what options they have for customers who are struggling. Many have them, and asking is allowed. And on a good day, set up protections for the harder ones. The institute recommends tools people can switch on while they're well to protect themselves when they're not, like a spending limit or a waiting period before big purchases.

Free help exists, and it's for you

You don't have to work out the rest on your own. Many countries have free, non-profit debt advice. In the UK there's MoneyHelper and StepChange. In the United States, non-profit credit counseling agencies, such as members of the NFCC, offer free or low-cost advice. Elsewhere, your national consumer protection agency is a good place to start. Be wary of any company that charges upfront fees or promises to make your debt disappear. Real advice doesn't start with a payment.

It helps to tell the advisor what makes this hard for you. You can ask for information in writing, for more time to read it, or to use email instead of the phone. Debt isn't a moral failure. It's a problem with a size and a shape, and problems with a shape can be worked on.

Quick answers

Are neurodivergent people more likely to be in debt?

The research points that way, across many conditions, not just one. In England, people with a mental health problem were about three and a half times as likely to be in problem debt, from about two and a half times for panic disorder to almost six times for OCD. Adults with ADHD in Sweden had more than six times the default risk by around age 40, and almost half of autistic adults in an Australian study struggled to make ends meet.

Why do I avoid looking at my debt?

Avoiding bad financial news is very common, and researchers call it the ostrich effect. With anxiety or depression, or after years of criticism about money, a balance can feel like a judgment on you, so looking away protects you in the moment. The cost is that fees and interest keep growing while you don't look.

How do I start getting out of debt if I'm neurodivergent?

Start small. In one short session, ideally with someone beside you, write down each debt with the lender, the amount, the minimum payment and the due date. Then stop the leaks with automatic minimum payments and cancelled subscriptions, ask lenders what options they have if you're struggling, and get free advice from a non-profit debt service. On a good day, set up protections for the harder days.

Is there an app that helps neurodivergent people manage debt?

Finiverse is a personal finance app designed for neurodivergent people, including those with ADHD, anxiety, depression, autism and dyscalculia. It shows a safe-to-spend number after upcoming bills and savings, sends payment reminders you can mark as paid, finds subscriptions you may have forgotten, and lets you hide balances on hard days.
