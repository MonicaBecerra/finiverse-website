Down Syndrome and Money: Independence With Support, Not Control

Published: October 23, 2026 · By Monica Becerra · 11 min read

The coffee costs $3.80. He already has a five-dollar bill in his hand, the one he folded this morning for exactly this. The barista looks straight past him, at his mother, and asks: “Cash or card?” Nobody meant any harm. But in that half second, the decision moved from the person paying to the person standing next to him. Many adults with Down syndrome live that small moment again and again: at the bank, at the phone shop, at the front desk of the doctor's office. This article is for them, and for the families who want to help without taking the wallet away.

In short: Many adults with Down syndrome can make some or all of their own money decisions, especially when information is explained clearly and someone they trust is there to help. The law in many countries now starts from that idea: capacity is presumed, and support comes before anyone decides for the person. Money skills can keep growing at any age, through real practice with real money. The family's role is to support decisions, not to make them.

Deciding about money isn't all or nothing

Many families carry a yes-or-no question around for years: can my daughter handle money or not? Research suggests it's the wrong question. In a study in Cambridge, researchers compared the financial decisions of 30 adults with mild intellectual disabilities with two groups from the general population. On average the first group scored lower, but the differences were not clear-cut, and many of them could make at least some of their own financial decisions. The study wasn't only about Down syndrome, and not every person with Down syndrome has a mild intellectual disability, but the lesson travels well.

The most useful finding was where people got stuck. In all three groups, the hardest steps were understanding the information and reasoning with it. That matters, because understanding is exactly what good support can improve. A contract written in plain words, a price shown as something familiar, an extra day to think: none of that takes the decision away. It makes the decision possible. So the real question isn't whether someone can manage money. It's which decisions they can make alone today, which ones they can make with help, and what that help looks like.

What the law says now

The law has been moving in the same direction. In England and Wales, the Mental Capacity Act starts from five principles. A person must be assumed to have capacity unless it's shown otherwise. Nobody can be treated as unable to decide until all practicable steps to help them have been tried without success. And making an unwise decision is not, on its own, a reason to treat someone as unable to decide. That last one is worth reading twice. Adults without disabilities make unwise money decisions every day, and nobody takes their bank card away.

At the international level, Article 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities says disabled people have legal capacity on an equal basis with everyone else. In 2014, the UN committee that oversees the Convention explained that this means moving away from systems where someone decides for the person, toward supported decision-making, where the person decides with help. Several Spanish-speaking countries have rewritten their laws around that idea. Spain ended court incapacitation and guardianship for disabled adults in 2021. Colombia did away with interdiction in 2019 and created support agreements, where the person chooses who helps them and with which decisions. Mexico abolished the state of interdiction in 2023, in its national code of civil and family procedure. The details vary by country, and this isn't legal advice, but the direction is clear: the person decides, with as much support as they need.

Number skills keep growing in adulthood

A common belief is that if someone didn't learn money at school, the chance has passed. Two Australian researchers, Faragher and Brown, argued the opposite. They observed and interviewed five adults with Down syndrome, each in two different settings, and looked at numeracy through the lens of quality of life. Their conclusion was that number skills can keep developing throughout life when they're built around what matters to the person, with a personal numeracy plan, and with family and support workers also teaching, not only helping. It was a small study, but it matches what many families see.

In practice, that means learning with real money in real places. A weekly amount in a wallet, kept apart from rent and bills, teaches more than any worksheet. So does a shopping list with prices written next to each item, paying at the same corner shop every week, and counting the change together at home afterwards instead of at a busy register. If numbers are especially hard, much of what I wrote about dyscalculia and money applies here too: compare prices with things the person already knows, and give time instead of pressure.

Cash, cards and the space in between

Cash has one big advantage: you can see it go. When the five-dollar bill leaves your hand, the money is visibly gone, and what's left is right there in the wallet. A card hides all of that. A tap at the till feels the same whether it costs a little or a lot. For many people, starting with cash and moving to a card later, or using both side by side, works better than going straight to a card.

When a card does come in, it can come in gently. Some families keep two accounts: one for rent and bills, handled together, and one for everyday spending that fully belongs to the person, with only the weekly amount moved into it. Many banks also let you set spending limits or switch off online payments on a card. And it helps to agree on one simple rule in advance, while everyone is calm: if anyone asks for money, a password or a code, by phone, by message or in person, pause and call someone you trust first. Scams depend on hurry. A rule made ahead of time gives the person something solid to hold on to.

For families: help that doesn't take over

If you're family, your help is probably a big part of why things work. The hard part is that the line between supporting and deciding is easy to cross without noticing. A few habits keep you on the right side of it. Talk to the person, not about them, including at the bank. Explain choices in plain words with real examples, then wait for the answer instead of filling the silence. And let small mistakes happen with small money. Spending the whole week's amount on Saturday is how most of us learned about Sunday.

It also helps to sort decisions together, out loud and ideally in writing. Some are fully theirs: snacks, gifts, clothes, a new game. Some are made together: a phone contract, a trip, anything that repeats every month. And a few need a second person every time, like loans, signing contracts or sharing bank details. Write down who helps with what, and look at it again every year, because the list should get shorter as confidence grows. In Colombia, that's the logic of a formal support agreement. At home, it can simply be a page on the fridge.

Quick answers

Can people with Down syndrome manage their own money?

Many can manage some or all of their own money, especially with clear information and support from someone they trust. Research with adults with mild intellectual disabilities found that many could make at least some of their own financial decisions, and that the hardest part for everyone was understanding the information, which support can improve. The useful question is which decisions a person can make alone, which with help, and what help they need.

What is supported decision-making?

Supported decision-making means the person makes their own decisions, with help from people they choose to understand information, weigh options and communicate their choice. It replaces the older model where someone else decides for them. The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities backs this approach, and countries such as Spain, Colombia and Mexico have reformed their laws around it.

How can I help my adult child with Down syndrome with money without taking control?

Talk to them, not about them, and explain choices in plain words with real examples. Let small mistakes happen with small amounts, such as a weekly spending amount kept apart from bills. Agree together which decisions are theirs alone, which you make together and which always need a second person, write it down, and review it as their confidence grows.
