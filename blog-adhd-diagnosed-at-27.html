Diagnosed with ADHD at 27: My Story With Money (and Why I Built Finiverse)

Published: November 4, 2026 · By Monica Becerra · 7 min read

I have travelled, worked for international companies and moved abroad on my own to study a master's. I also have ADHD, and I wasn't diagnosed until I was 27. From the outside it can look like I had everything under control. From the inside, every one of those things took a lot of work that nobody could see.

In short: I was diagnosed with ADHD at 27. I still travelled, worked internationally and moved abroad alone for a master's, but none of it was easy, and living abroad forced me to get strict with money. What helped most is the 3-month rule: when I want something, I wait three months, and if I still want it, I buy it. Finiverse started as a spreadsheet my sister asked for and became an app built the way my brain works. Money still overwhelms me sometimes, and I'm still learning not to punish myself for it.

A diagnosis at 27

Getting diagnosed as an adult doesn't erase the years before. It gives them a new name. A lot of things I had always blamed on myself started to make sense: why some days I could do ten things at once and other days I couldn't start one, why I lost track of things I truly cared about, why money felt so slippery.

If you also found out as an adult, you probably know that mix of relief and "so that's why." The diagnosis didn't fix anything by itself. But it changed the question from "what's wrong with me?" to "what does my brain need?"

It didn't stop me. It made everything cost more

I don't want this to sound like a success story where ADHD was a small detail. It wasn't. Travelling, working for international companies, moving to another country alone to study: I did all of it with ADHD, and each one took more effort than it looked like from the outside.

I'm writing this because, years ago, I would have liked to read that you can have ADHD and still do the big things. It may cost you more. It's still possible.

Living abroad: I starved my impulse to buy

Living abroad on my own meant I had to be much stricter with money. Basically, I starved my impulse to buy. Not because I stopped wanting things. ADHD doesn't work like that. The urge was still there, as loud as ever. I just stopped feeding it.

With ADHD, impulse buying isn't a lack of willpower. The reward in front of you feels bigger than anything that comes later, and emotions arrive fast. I explain the science in the article on ADHD spending triggers. So "just don't buy it" never worked for me. What worked was putting time between the urge and the decision.

The 3-month rule

Since 2020 I've used one simple rule. When I want something that isn't a need, I don't buy it right then. I write it down and wait three months. If after three months I still want it, and it fits, I buy it, without guilt. Most of the time, by then, I've forgotten about it, or I look at it and feel nothing.

It works for my brain because it doesn't ask me to say no. It asks me to say "not yet," which is much easier to hear. The urge gets written down, so it isn't lost. And three months is long enough for the rush of the moment to fade, so the calmer version of me gets to decide.

A spreadsheet for my sister that became an app

Finiverse started with a small request. My sister asked me to make her an Excel to organise her money. And my ADHD brain said: what if I make an app instead? If you have ADHD, you know that moment. The idea arrives, and suddenly it's the only thing you can think about.

I didn't know how to code. I learned Xcode with Google and YouTube, one error at a time. Over time I started leaning on AI too, because I wasn't an expert, and it could see mistakes that I couldn't. It became a second pair of eyes.

Somewhere along the way I realised something. I was building the app the way my brain works. One calm number instead of ten. A pause before buying. Envelopes and colours instead of columns of figures. Reminders that remember for me. It wasn't a business plan. It was me making something I could actually use.

I still get overwhelmed

I'd love to end this by saying I've figured it out. I haven't. Money is still a topic that stresses me out. Some days I open my accounts and feel overwhelmed before I've read a single number. And I still catch myself punishing myself: for a purchase, for a forgotten payment, for not being "better" at this by now.

I'm working on it. I'm learning that feeling overwhelmed doesn't mean I'm failing, and that punishing myself has never fixed a single money problem. It only makes me avoid looking, and that makes the next problem bigger. So I try to treat myself the way I'd treat a friend: with patience, and with one small next step.

That's also why Finiverse doesn't scold. There's no harsh red, no shame. On hard days you can hide your balances. And when you add a mood to a purchase, it isn't to judge it. It's to understand it.

If you have ADHD and money stresses you out

You're not bad with money. Your brain needs a different system, and you're allowed to build one that fits you, even if it looks strange to everyone else. Mine started with a three-month wait and a spreadsheet for my sister. Yours can start with something just as small. If you want more ideas, this guide to ADHD and money is a good next read.

Quick answers

Can you be diagnosed with ADHD as an adult?

Yes. Many people are diagnosed as adults, often after years of thinking their struggles were personal failings. I was diagnosed at 27. If you recognise yourself in ADHD, a doctor or psychologist who assesses adults can help.

What is the 3-month rule for spending?

It's a simple waiting rule. When you want something that isn't a need, you write it down and wait three months before buying it. If you still want it and it fits your budget, you buy it without guilt. Often the urge has passed by then. It suits ADHD because it says "not yet" instead of "no."

How do you control impulse buying with ADHD?

Willpower alone rarely works, because ADHD makes the reward in front of you feel bigger than anything later. What helps is putting time and steps between the urge and the purchase: a waiting rule, removing saved cards from shopping sites, and noticing which moods tend to come before spending.
